Build your football knowledge

These tips apply across plays. Open a topic to learn the position, understand the words you hear, and build good habits.

Think before the snap. Play fast after it.

This is why you study formations. Before the ball moves, recognize the offense, hear the call, find your key, and know your job. Picture your response to a run, a pass, or a QB keeper.

When the ball is snapped, trust your preparation. Read your key, find the ball, and react. You want quick, confident action—not a long conversation in your head while the play passes you.

Reps build instinct. The more you recognize these looks, the faster the right response becomes. Play with full effort and trust your assignment. Fast does not mean guessing or chasing a fake; it means seeing your read and going.

Listen for River or Laso

River = right. Laso = left. Start by looking for the wide side—the side with more room between the ball and the sideline. For the alignment rule in this guide, expect that to be the strong side.

Left hash → wide side right → expect River.
Right hash → wide side left → expect Laso.

Always listen for the FS. Your free safety makes the strength call. The wide side gives you a starting expectation; the FS’s call confirms the side your defense is using. Follow that call and your assignment, even if your first guess was different.

Ball in the middle? Neither side is wider. Read the formation and listen for the FS to declare River or Laso. If the formation is balanced, don’t pick a side on your own.

Football talk & things to know

You’re new to this. You don’t have to know every word yet. Start with these habits, then connect them to the call you’re learning.

Beat the block. Keep your edge.

“Shed the block” means get free of the blocker. Use your taught hand placement, keep a balanced base, and create space so he cannot stay attached to you. Find the ball and separate when you can make the play.

Don’t win the block and lose the edge. If you are force, protect the outside escape lane as you work free. Don’t duck inside just to get around someone and let the runner turn the corner. Keep the run directed toward your help; the corner has his own job.

“Outside arm free” is a cue you may hear for an edge fit: don’t let a blocker trap the side you need to protect. Use the technique and leverage your coaches assign. Against a run away, get free and pursue without abandoning a keeper or cutback responsibility too early.

Run to the ball—with a purpose

“Pursuit” means getting to the ball. Once your read tells you to go, run with effort and take an angle that meets the runner. Don’t trail directly behind him if you can cut off his path.

“Rally” or “swarm” means teammates closing together. Keep your lane, stay alert for a cutback, and help finish the play. If a teammate has the runner wrapped, help within the rules; don’t pile on after the whistle.

Effort lasts until the whistle. Hustle to help, but stop contact when the play is dead. A scrambling QB may still pass, so “run to the ball” does not mean abandoning coverage before your call allows it.

Talk through your read: run or pass

Calling “run!” or “pass!” can be a useful habit once your assigned key gives you that read. It makes you recognize what you see and can help your teammates. Use the team’s communication words.

Say it in your head while learning, or out loud when that is your team’s practice. Don’t shout a guess from the formation. Keep watching: a fake handoff, screen, or block-and-release can change the picture.

No sack? Still affect the throw.

“Get your hands up” means challenge the passing lane. If you are rushing and the QB is about to throw before you can reach him, raise your arms to obstruct or tip the pass. You can affect the play without a sack.

Keep your balance and rush lane. Don’t jump past a QB who can run around you. Watch the throwing motion; don’t stop your rush early just because the sack looks difficult.

Get low. Wrap up. Finish.

“Get low” means bend at your knees and hips into a strong, balanced position. It does not mean lower your head, dive at knees, or lead with your helmet. Keep your eyes up and your head out of the contact.

“Wrap up” means secure the runner with your arms. Use the shoulder contact and aiming point your coaches teach, wrap and squeeze, and keep your feet working through the tackle. Don’t just bump him and hope he falls.

“Break down” means get under control near the runner. Shorten your steps, stay balanced, and avoid flying past him. Good tackling is controlled technique and a secure finish, not a contest to make the biggest hit.

Practice QB vs. game QB

In practice, follow the contact rules. If your QB is protected, tag off or stop exactly as the coaches tell you. Don’t tackle him to prove you could have made a sack.

In a game, rush with intent. Disrupt the backfield. Finish the sack. When you reach the QB with the ball, commit to a hard, controlled tackle: use your coached shoulder contact, wrap him up, and finish. Make him hurry his reads and throws. Bring speed, power, and technique—not hesitation.

Know when to stop. No late shot after the throw, no helmet-led hit, and no contact after a slide, out of bounds, or the whistle. Play hard while the play is live; keep your discipline when it ends.

New to football? Start here

Your key: the player your coaches tell you to watch first. It could be a receiver, TE, or another blocker. Look at that player first; use the ball to confirm the play.

Block: an offensive player tries to keep you or a teammate away from the ball. Release: he leaves his starting spot to run a route. A block can signal a run or screen; a release can signal a pass. Neither is proof by itself.

Play-action: the offense fakes a run, then passes. Screen: a short pass with blockers in front. QB keeper: the QB keeps the ball instead of handing it off. Scramble: he leaves the pocket during a pass play and may still throw.

Force: keep the ball from escaping outside and turn it toward help. Spill: make the runner bounce outside toward a force defender. Alley: the space between the inside defense and the outside support.

Leverage: which side of a blocker or receiver you must protect. Gap: the space between blockers. Contain: keep the ball carrier from escaping around the outside.

Don’t chase the QB with your eyes on every call. In man, start with your receiver. In zone, use the coverage’s receiver and QB eye rules. On a run read, follow your assigned blocking key. Your eyes change with your job.

Remember these three things

Keep your edge. When you are the force player, don’t let the runner get outside you. Turn him back toward your teammates.

Read before you chase. Watch your assigned receiver or blocker. A fake handoff can turn into a pass.

Trust your call. Your coaches’ call tells you where to go and what to do. Stay with your job.

Who’s who

SS: you, the strong safety. WS: weak safety. FS: free safety; makes the strength call when it isn’t obvious. River means right. Laso means left. CB: corner. E: defensive end. N: nose. Defensive T: tackle. M/W: inside linebackers. Offensive T/G/C: tackle, guard, center. X/Z/H/Y/R: eligible receivers. TE: tight end. QB/RB/FB: quarterback, running back, fullback.

Count eligible receivers outside inward on each side: No. 1 is widest; No. 2 is next inside. Field means the wider side of the field; boundary means the shorter side. Strength comes from the receivers or extra blockers, using your team’s rule. Force turns a runner inside; spill makes him bounce outside.

Get better one rep at a time

Before the snap: Say your job to yourself: “I’m force,” “I’m rushing,” “I’ve got curl/flat,” or “I’ve got No. 2.” Then find the player you’re supposed to read.

At the snap: Watch your key. Don’t guess from the formation or stare only at the quarterback. Make your first steps match what you see and what the call asks you to do.

After the rep: Check three things: Was I in the right spot? Did I read the right player? Did I keep my assignment? Pick one thing to improve on the next rep.

Coaching references & how to use this guide

TCU foundation: The 1999 Gary Patterson TCU defensive install provides the historical reference for field/formation strength, three-safety structure, receiver counting and outside-safety coverage/pressure examples. TCU used multiple coverages and adjustments. The diagrams here isolate your job; they are not complete eleven-player defensive calls.

The four study buttons: Power means the run-support interpretation discussed for this guide; it is not a verified definition of your team’s call or a universal TCU safety call. Blitz illustrates an outside safety rush, Zone a curl/flat assignment, and Man a No. 2 matchup. Your actual front, coverage and checks decide your key, leverage, alignment and help. River = right, Laso = left and the FS strength call are team terms supplied by the family, not names verified in the historical TCU install. This guide illustrates right/left from the defender’s viewpoint. Confirm that the coaches use that same viewpoint for River/Laso before applying the diagram to a team call.

Youth and high-school technique: USA Football’s Shoulder Tackling system supports the approach, leverage, shoulder contact and finish principles. Its Blocking & Defeating Blocks system supports balanced contact, use of the hands and creating separation. Practice these techniques with your coaches; the diagrams teach decisions, not contact drills.

Formation labels: Run-leaning and pass-leaning describe the personnel and spacing in the example, not a prediction or a measured team tendency. No formation guarantees a play. UIL football rules and guidelines are the reference for Texas school football rules.
